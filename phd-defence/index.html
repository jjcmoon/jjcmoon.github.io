About the thesis

Neural probabilistic logic programming with circuits and embeddings

Supervisor: Prof. dr. Luc De Raedt

Modern artificial intelligence excels at learning from data. A neural network can for instance recognise traffic lights and pedestrians in images. Yet a safe self-driving car should also know that it must stop at a red light. This thesis investigates systems in which people can write down such rules instead of expecting the computer to learn them.

Neural networks learn from raw data, while symbolic programs express human-readable rules. Neurosymbolic artificial intelligence combines these two in a single system. In the car example, the learning component may estimate the chance that a traffic light is red. The rule-based component then uses this estimate to decide how to behave. Such systems can require fewer examples, are easier to adjust, and are more trustworthy.

The first part of the thesis concerns the computation in neurosymbolic systems. This computation is difficult as there are too many different possibilities to evaluate. We first develop a single method to perform many different calculations on a neurosymbolic system. Next, we analyse when answers are too expensive to calculate, and propose a new way to estimate answers with guarantees. Finally, we also rearrange rule-based calculations to fit them on the same computer chips used by neural networks. In some tests, this makes them up to ten thousand times faster.

The second part of the thesis concerns the meaning of the rules we wrote down. For rules, different symbols such as chair and cat, or cat and kitten are just as unrelated. A neural system on the other hand can quantify how similar concepts are. This thesis investigates on how to use this similarity when reasoning with rules. Together, our contributions make it easier to specify a neurosymbolic systems and makes their computation faster.
